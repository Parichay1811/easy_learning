Posts written to be **found**. LinkedIn search (and Google, which indexes public posts)
matches the words in a post, so each post owns one phrase people actually type, such as
"help a child with dyslexia at home" or "ADHD homework tips". That phrase goes in the hook
(first 140 characters), comes back once naturally in the body, and gets matching
hashtags. The posts give real, practical value first. Learnigo appears once, at the end, as
one option among the tips, never the point.

Rules from `RULES.md` still apply: statement hook, no link in the body (first comment), 0–3
niche hashtags at the end, a specific question to close, no medical claims.

S3–S6 go out as **image posts**: the poster from `linkedin-posters/` (made by `tools/posters.mjs`) with the post text as its caption.
Carousels (S1, S2) are PDF "document" posts: upload the PDF from `linkedin-carousels/`,
give it the title shown, and paste the post text.

**Facts check.** The tips are general good practice for learning at home and in class, not
medical advice. Before posting S3 (myths), have a teacher or SENCO you trust read it.

```
7 small ways to help a child with dyslexia at home, and none of them need a tutor.

Most advice for parents is either too clinical or too vague. These are the things families tell us actually change the evening:

1. Read to them, not just with them. Hearing the story keeps the love of books alive while reading catches up.
2. Let them answer out loud. Knowing the answer and writing it are two different skills.
```

## S2 · Carousel · "ADHD homework tips"

**Search phrase:** ADHD homework tips · **Hashtags:** #ADHD #ADHDKids #Parenting
**PDF:** `linkedin-carousels/S2-adhd-homework.pdf` · **Document title:** `ADHD homework tips: 6 changes for calmer evenings`

```
ADHD homework tips that make evenings calmer, from families who have tried everything else.

Homework fights usually aren't about effort. They're about a format that asks a busy brain to sit still for too long with too much on the page.

1. Short bursts: 10 minutes on, a 2-minute movement break, repeat.
2. One task in sight. Put the rest of the worksheet away.
3. Start with an easy win. Momentum is real.
4. Let them move: standing, a wobble cushion, walking while they spell out loud.
5. Make time visible but optional. A timer helps some children and stresses others; let them choose.
6. Finish on a success, even a small one, so tomorrow starts well.

None of this is a diagnosis or a treatment. It's just homework shaped around the child instead of the other way round.
```

## S3 · Text · "dyslexia myths"

**Search phrase:** dyslexia myths · **Hashtags:** #Dyslexia #Neurodiversity #Education
**Poster:** `linkedin-posters/S3-dyslexia-myths.png` (attach as the post image; the text below is the caption)

```
5 dyslexia myths parents still hear at the school gate, and what's closer to the truth.

Myth 1: "It means they're not clever." Dyslexia isn't a measure of intelligence. Many dyslexic children are quick, creative thinkers who find written words hard.

Myth 2: "It's just seeing letters backwards." Reversing letters is common in all young children. Dyslexia is mostly about connecting sounds to letters, and about speed.

Myth 3: "They'll grow out of it." Children don't outgrow dyslexia; they grow into strategies that work for them, and those strategies can be learned early.

Myth 4: "They just need to try harder." Most are already working twice as hard. What helps is changing the format: audio, time, fewer words on a page.

Myth 5: "Reading slowly means understanding less." When a passage is read aloud, many children show they understood all of it.

If you're worried about your child, a teacher, SENCO or qualified assessor is the right first conversation.
```

```
5 brain breaks for kids that take two minutes and need no equipment.

Back after the holidays, attention is the scarcest thing in the room. A short movement break often gets more out of the next 20 minutes than pushing on.

1. Freeze frame: move until the music stops, then hold a shape.
2. Mirror me: one child leads slow movements, everyone copies.
3. Air spelling: write this week's words in the air with an elbow, then a foot.
4. Count and reach: touch high, low, left, right as the class counts in twos or fives.
5. Traffic lights: green walk on the spot, amber slow motion, red stand still.
```

## S5 · Text · "inclusive classroom ideas"

**Search phrase:** inclusive classroom ideas · **Hashtags:** #InclusiveEducation #SEND #Teachers
**Poster:** `linkedin-posters/S5-inclusive-classroom.png`

```
6 inclusive classroom ideas that cost nothing and help every child, not only the ones with a label.

1. Read every question aloud, every time. It removes a barrier for some and costs the others nothing.
2. Offer two ways to answer: say it or write it.
3. Fewer words per slide. One idea, big text, space around it.
4. Make the clock optional. Untimed practice first; timed only when they're ready.
5. Build in movement: stand to answer, walk to a corner to vote.
6. Celebrate personal bests, not ranks. "Beat your own score" keeps everyone in the game.

Inclusive design is good design. When the way in fits more children, more children stay in.
```
